Global Warming Has Accelerated:
Are the United Nations and the Public Well-Informed?

writen by: James E. Hansen,Pushker Kharecha,Makiko Sato,George Tselioudis,Joseph Kelly,Susanne E. Bauer,Reto Ruedy,Eunbi Jeong,Qinjian Jin,Eric Rignot,Isabella Velicogna,Mark R. Schoeberl,Karina von Schuckmann,Joshua Amponsem,Junji Cao,Anton Keskinen,Jing Li &Anni Pokela

Global temperature increased by more than 0.4°C (0.7°F) in the past two years, peaking in August 2024 at +1.6°C compared to the early 20th century average (1880-1920). This rise was partly due to a tropical El Niño event, but the magnitude was unexpectedly large. Scientists found that the other half of the warming was caused by reduced aerosol emissions from ships, a regulation imposed in 2020 to improve human health. Aerosols help form clouds that reflect sunlight, cooling the Earth. With fewer aerosols, there are fewer clouds, leading to more sunlight absorption and increased warming. Ships are a major aerosol source in the North Pacific and North Atlantic Oceans. Satellite data showed the largest effects in these regions. The United Nations Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) may have underestimated the cooling effect of aerosols and thus the climate sensitivity.

The reduction in ship aerosols will continue to impact global warming, even as the tropical climate shifts to a cooler La Niña phase. We expect global temperatures to remain around or above +1.5°C for the next few years, supporting our findings on the recent rapid warming. High sea surface temperatures and ocean hotspots will persist, harming coral reefs and marine life. For humans, the most noticeable effects will be more frequent and severe climate extremes. This includes stronger tropical storms, tornadoes, and thunderstorms, leading to more extreme floods. Higher global temperatures will also intensify heat waves and droughts, including sudden "flash droughts" in areas with otherwise sufficient rainfall.

Polar climate change has the greatest long-term effect on humanity, with impacts accelerated by the recent rise in global temperature. Our findings show that polar ice melt and freshwater injection into the North Atlantic Ocean are higher than previously estimated. Due to accelerated global warming, this melt will continue to increase. Consequently, the shutdown of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is likely within the next 20-30 years unless actions are taken to reduce global warming. This contradicts the conclusions of the IPCC. If the AMOC shuts down, it will cause significant issues, including a sea level rise of several meters. Therefore, we describe the AMOC shutdown as the “point of no return.”

Global warming has accelerated significantly since 2010, increasing by more than 50% compared to the 1970-2010 rate of 0.18°C per decade. Earth is now warmer than at any time in the past 11,700 years, and it is at least as warm as during the extreme warm period 120,000 years ago. The recent moderate El Niño contributed to a 0.4°C increase in global temperature, a much greater warming than during previous strong El Niños. This rapid warming has puzzled scientists, who have struggled to explain it with known mechanisms. However, we conclude that human-made greenhouse gases and aerosols are the primary drivers of this observed global temperature increase, including a significant rise in sea surface temperature that has led to the widely discussed 1.5°C temperature threshold being effectively breached.
